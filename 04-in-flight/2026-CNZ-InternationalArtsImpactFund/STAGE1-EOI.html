New Zealand based arts organisation

Anamata Kāhui Limited is a registered New Zealand company, NZBN 9429052960734, trading as Anamata Records. The label is Māori led, governed with a kaitiaki rōpū, and works to a documented cultural review process with Precious Clark of Maurea, who has worked with us since 2024.

International experience and capability

Our international practice runs artist to artist rather than through agency representation. Two bilingual waiata, まだ終わっちゃいない (Not over yet) and ただ声一つ (One Voice), hold te reo Māori and Japanese inside the same recording and are released to international platforms. Our collaborating artist is Ryo Suzuki. The catalogue also carries Te Tinihanga (The Cunning), Wairua Piri Ta'i (Mita o te Hauāuru) and Te Whakamātautau (The Diver’s Test), each composed from Ngāti Kahungunu kōrero tuku iho. A third collaborative work is in production.

Evidence of international demand

Demand shows up in three places. A Japanese collaborator who has stayed across repeat releases rather than one session. A Japanese entity now in formation, which gives the label a contracting counterpart in market and the standing to hold its own international rights. And two Japanese partners, Ryo Suzuki and Hikaru Yamaguchi, already working with us across recording and live presentation. We are building the structure in Japan that this programme will run through, and we are doing it ahead of any funding decision.

Current international strategy

Our international strategy is written as the three year programme set out below: a Japanese market built on bilingual waiata, a formal entity, named partners, a live pathway, and a published cross Indigenous creative protocol. A standalone strategy document with market analysis, partner detail and a full budget accompanies our Stage Two application.

A summary of your international experience and goals

Anamata Records is the music division of Anamata Kāhui Limited, a Māori led label in Aotearoa New Zealand driving the Māori futurism movement in Aotearoa’s music industry. The label was founded by Ngāika Smith, a composer, producer and researcher with whakapapa to Ngāi Tahu, Ngāti Kahungunu and Ngāti Awa, and is governed with a kaitiaki rōpū. Our waiata are composed in te reo Māori, arranged around taonga pūoro and contemporary production, and reviewed for cultural integrity before release.

What we have built internationally

Our international work started with a single collaboration and has grown into a relationship. Working with the Japanese artist Ryo Suzuki, we have released two waiata that hold te reo Māori and Japanese as equal voices in one recording rather than as a translation of one another: まだ終わっちゃいない (Not over yet) and ただ声一つ (One Voice). A third collaborative work is in production.

Making those records taught us what international collaboration actually costs a small Indigenous language label. Coordinating across time zones. Splitting rights across two jurisdictions. Holding two sets of cultural protocols inside one studio session so that neither becomes decoration. We learned it by trial, at our own expense, and we would rather the next Māori label did not have to.

What we are building in Japan now

Anamata Kāhui Limited is establishing a formal Japanese presence. I am in Japan as this Expression of Interest is filed, working through the formation of a Japanese entity that will hold the label’s international rights and act as our contracting counterpart in market. Alongside the entity, two Japanese partners are working with us across recording and live presentation: Ryo Suzuki, our collaborating artist on the bilingual catalogue, and Hikaru Yamaguchi. Those partnerships are the route by which our catalogue reaches Japanese audiences, rooms and release channels.

That is the difference between this application and a proposal on paper. The entity, the partners and the working relationship exist. We are building the structure with our own resources, ahead of any funding decision, because the music only travels as far as the structure allows it to.

The goal

To make Anamata Records a permanent Māori presence in the Japanese music market. That means an entity that can contract in Japan, a catalogue that moves in two languages, a live pathway that repeats each year, and a written protocol for cross Indigenous collaboration that other Māori artists can take up and use without repeating our tuition.

Japan is the right first market for three reasons. It is the second largest recorded music market in the world. It rewards craft led records and long relationships over short campaigns, which is the shape of our practice. And we already have the collaborator, the language pairing and now the entity to enter it properly rather than as a visitor.

Why now

Three things are converging in 2027 and will not hold for long. The Japanese entity completes its formation. Two Japanese partners, Ryo Suzuki and Hikaru Yamaguchi, are engaged and ready to programme work. And the bilingual catalogue is moving, with a third collaborative record in production. A market entry run in 2027 lands on all three. A market entry run later lands on none of them.

The three year programme

Year one, 2027: Build and record. Formalise the Japanese entity and the partnership relationships into written agreements covering language use, cultural review, rights division and mana over the work. Compose and record a bilingual EP of four waiata in te reo Māori and Japanese, with taonga pūoro written into lead roles. Register the works with APRA and their Japanese counterparts. Publish the international strategy as a standing document.

Year two, 2028: Present. Take the programme into the Japanese market in person: two performances for Japanese industry in Tokyo and one regional centre, six or more meetings with Japanese labels, publishers and festival programmers, and a co-writing residency in Aotearoa hosting our Japanese partners on Kahungunu and Ngāi Tahu whenua. Release two further waiata.

Year three, 2029: Consolidate. Release a full length collaborative album in both markets. Present at a major international festival. Publish the cross Indigenous creative protocol framework openly and hand the model to a second Māori act, so the pathway outlives this grant and stops depending on us.

What this investment changes

It turns a relationship built on instinct into an institution with a paper trail, so the collaboration survives a change of personnel on either side. It moves us from releasing waiata into Japan to building a Japanese audience for them, which is a different job and needs people on the ground. And it publishes the method, so the next Māori label enters an international market with a protocol rather than a guess.
