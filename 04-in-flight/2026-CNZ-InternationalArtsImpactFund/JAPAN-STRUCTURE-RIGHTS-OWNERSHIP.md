## The recommendation in one line

Establish a single member gōdō gaisha owned 100 percent by Anamata Kāhui Limited, hold every master recording, every composition and every trade mark in New Zealand, and let the Japanese company operate only under an exclusive Japan territory licence. Pay the Japanese partner for what he contributes rather than giving him ownership.

---

## The ownership answer

**Yes, 100 percent is achievable, and it is the standard route.** Japan permits full foreign ownership of a gōdō gaisha, and recorded music is not one of the restricted sectors (broadcasting, telecoms, airlines and some primary industries are restricted; music is not). A New Zealand company can therefore hold the entire entity with no Japanese co-member and no local shareholder requirement. There is no legal mechanism forcing a local partner, and nothing in the structure needs one.

**Why a gōdō gaisha (合同会社, GK) rather than a kabushiki gaisha (株式会社, KK).** A GK is the Japanese limited liability company. It is formed by its members, registered at the Legal Affairs Bureau, and run by an executive manager chosen from among the members. The manager may be an individual or a corporation. It has no board, no statutory auditor and no share register to administer. Large foreign groups now use GKs as their Japanese subsidiaries, so it carries no prestige penalty. A KK is the right vehicle when you need shareholders, a board or a listing. You need none of those.

| Vehicle | Can a New Zealand company own 100 percent | Who represents it | Ceremony | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single member GK (合同会社) | Yes | Executive manager appointed by the member, individual or corporation | Lowest. One member, no board, no auditor | Recommended |
| KK (株式会社) | Yes | Representative director required, auditor once there are multiple directors | Higher cost and formality | Not needed yet |
| Japanese branch of the NZ company | Yes, and it is not a separate legal entity | Branch manager | No incorporation, but no liability separation and Japanese profits are taxed in Japan | Not recommended |
| Joint venture with a Japanese partner | No, by definition diluted | Shared | Cheapest to start | Rejected, see below |

## The four levers that keep you at 100 percent

1. **The member is Anamata Kāhui Limited.** Sole member, no co-investor. Ownership of the entity is total from day one.
2. **Write the transfer restriction into the articles of incorporation.** In a GK, all members must consent to any transfer of ownership unless the articles provide otherwise. Draft the articles so member consent is required. That locks the register against any future transfer you have not agreed to.
3. **Appoint and remove the executive manager by member resolution.** The manager runs the company day to day; the member can replace them. Control of the entity never depends on the manager's goodwill.
4. **Put nothing of value inside the Japanese company.** No masters, no publishing, no trade marks that matter. This is the lever most people miss: if the Japanese entity holds the trade marks and the entity is ever sold, wound up or claimed against, the name goes with it. Register the Anamata Records marks in Japan **in the name of the New Zealand entity**, with the Japanese company as a licensed user.

## The rights architecture

**Split the asset from the operation.** The Japanese company is an operating and market development business. It is not an asset holder.

| Asset | Where it sits | What the Japanese company gets |
|---|---|---|
| Compositions (publishing) | Copyright assigned or exclusively licensed to Anamata Kāhui Limited in New Zealand | A licence to exploit in the Japan territory only |
| Master recordings | Owned by Anamata Kāhui Limited in New Zealand | A licence to exploit in the Japan territory only |
| Trade marks | Registered in Japan in the New Zealand entity's name | A licensed user right |
| Japanese company | Owned 100 percent by Anamata Kāhui Limited | Its own trading margin and service fee |

**Make the licence do the work.** The exclusive Japan territory licence is the document that keeps ownership where you want it. Six clauses to insist on:

1. **Territory limited to Japan.** Everything else stays with the New Zealand entity.
2. **Defined term with renewal tied to performance.** Not perpetual by default.
3. **Rights reserved to the licensor.** No sub-licensing without written consent.
4. **Audit right**, exercisable annually.
5. **Automatic termination on insolvency** or on the entity ceasing to trade.
6. **Reversion of all rights** on termination, with a short notice and sell-off period.

**Then the money flows back.** The Japanese company earns a distribution margin or service fee in Japan, and remits a licence fee or royalty on rights income to New Zealand. Ownership never moves, and if the Japanese operation ever stopped trading the catalogue would not be inside it.

**Collection.** Register the works with APRA AMCOS in New Zealand. For Japanese collection, either route through APRA's international agreements or appoint a Japanese sub-publisher. A sub-publishing deal is territory limited and does not touch ownership.

**One item to confirm before the first invoice.** Japan levies withholding tax on royalties paid to non-residents, and the New Zealand and Japan double tax agreement provides a reduced rate for copyright royalties. I could not verify the current rate from here, so treat it as an open item for the accountant rather than a number to plan around.

## Paying the partner without giving equity

Ryo Suzuki is a collaborating artist and a partner to the organisation. That deserves real participation. It does not require equity, and giving equity before the entity exists and has been valued is the mistake to avoid.

| Tier | What it is | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A named creative role plus a defined share of Japan territory revenue, capped to the territory and to a defined term, with no claim on the master or the publishing | Default position. Do this now |
| 2 | A commission or agency fee on deals he introduces | For specific opportunities, alongside tier 1 |
| 3 | A capped minority interest **in the Japanese company only**, never in the New Zealand rights entity, with vesting and a buy-back option | Only once the entity exists and has been independently valued, and only if he is contributing capital or permanent capability |

Why not a joint venture: a joint venture hands over a permanent share of the masters in exchange for market access, introductions, studio relationships and language work. Every one of those is a service that can be contracted and paid for honestly, at a price, without transferring a perpetual asset. Keep the equity, buy the access.

## Sequence

1. **Settle the representative question first.** Registration needs a registered address, and banking needs a resident contact. This single decision shapes the rest.
2. **Confirm costs and the tax position** with a judicial scrivener (shihō shoshi) and the accountant: registration and licence tax, the royalty withholding rate under the double tax agreement, and the consumption tax treatment.
3. **File the articles of incorporation** with the transfer restriction and the member consent clause written in.
4. **Register at the Legal Affairs Bureau.**
5. **Open the corporate bank account.** Expect this to be the slowest step for a foreign owned entity. Japanese banks are cautious with newly incorporated foreign owned companies; many founders operate through international payment providers first and move to a domestic bank once there is trading history. Plan for it rather than being blocked by it.
6. **File the trade marks in Japan** in the New Zealand entity's name.
7. **Execute the licence agreement** and the partner agreement.
8. **Then release.** Nothing goes into the Japanese market before the rights position is documented.

## Verified facts and figures

- A GK is formed by articles of incorporation between its members and registered at the Legal Affairs Bureau. The executive manager may be an individual or a corporation. Members must consent to any transfer of ownership unless the articles say otherwise. (Companies Act of Japan, as summarised on the public record.)
- Large foreign groups, including Apple, Google and Amazon, use GKs for their Japanese operations.
- A company can be registered with as little as ¥1 in capital. (JETRO, setting up a business in Japan.)
- A KK can be incorporated for roughly ¥240,000 in taxes and notarisation fees at minimum capital. (Public record.)
- Registration and licence tax is commonly published as ¥150,000 minimum for a KK against ¥60,000 for a GK. **Confirm with the scrivener.**
- A newly established corporation with capital of ¥10 million or more must file and pay consumption tax from the start, while one below the threshold can be an exempt enterprise for its first periods. Setting capital deliberately under ¥10 million is a real cash flow decision, not an accident. (JETRO, tax section.)
- Japan's corporate income tax applies to a GK as it does to a KK; there is no pass through treatment in Japan.

## What to decide next

1. Who the representative in Japan is, and whether they are a member, a manager, or a contracted formation agent.
2. Capital amount, and therefore the consumption tax position.
3. Whether the rights holder stays as Anamata Kāhui Limited or moves into a dedicated New Zealand holding company. A dedicated rights company is tidier if Anamata Records is ever partly divested, and it costs little to set up now.
