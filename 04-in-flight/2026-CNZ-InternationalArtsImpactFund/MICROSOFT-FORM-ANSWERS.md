## Provide a summary of your international experience

Anamata Records is the music division of Anamata Kāhui Limited, NZBN 9429052960734, part of Te Kāhui Anamata. We are a Māori led label based in Aotearoa New Zealand, founded by Ngaika Smith, a composer, producer and researcher with whakapapa to Ngāi Tahu, Ngāti Kahungunu and Ngāti Awa, and governed with a kaitiaki rōpū. Our waiata are composed in te reo Māori, arranged around taonga pūoro and contemporary production, and reviewed for cultural integrity before release by Precious Clark of Maurea, who has worked with us since 2024.

Our international practice runs artist to artist rather than through agency representation, and it is built on one relationship that has held. Working with the Japanese artist Ryo Suzuki, we have released two waiata that hold te reo Māori and Japanese as equal voices inside a single recording rather than as a translation of one another: まだ終わっちゃいない (Not over yet) and ただ声一つ (One Voice). Both are live on international platforms including Apple Music and YouTube. A third collaborative work is in production, and Ryo Suzuki is now a partner to the organisation as well as our collaborating artist.

The wider catalogue includes Te Tinihanga (The Cunning), Wairua Piri Ta'i (Mita o te Hauāuru) and Te Whakamātautau (The Diver's Test), each composed from Ngāti Kahungunu kōrero tuku iho. Alongside those releases we hold six new waiata in development, bilingual between te reo Māori and Japanese, each with its own focus and each designed to stand as its own release.

We are in Japan now, working through the establishment of a Japanese market presence: the formation of a vehicle that Anamata Kāhui Limited would own outright, and the partnership terms that will sit around it. No entity has been established yet. The work is deliberate, and it is being done ahead of any funding decision, because the music only travels as far as the structure allows it to.

In 2026 Creative New Zealand invested $10,000 through the Organizations and Groups Development Fund for a twelve month programme embedding cultural competency and accessibility into our operations. That programme produced the cultural review process and the accessibility standards our international work now rests on, including trilingual release assets and haptic mixing guidance for tāngata whaikaha Māori.

What we have learned about international collaboration, we learned by trial and at our own expense: coordinating across time zones, splitting rights across two jurisdictions, and holding two sets of cultural protocols inside one studio session so that neither becomes decoration. We would rather the next Māori label did not have to pay that tuition.

## Provide a summary of your international goals

Our goal is a permanent, New Zealand owned Māori presence in the Japanese music market. Not a tour and not a one off collaboration, but a structure that can contract, release, collect and repeat, and that we own.

Ownership and control stay in Aotearoa New Zealand. The Japanese vehicle will be a single member gōdō gaisha, the Japanese limited liability company, owned 100 percent by Anamata Kāhui Limited. Japan permits full foreign ownership of a gōdō gaisha and recorded music is not a restricted sector, so no Japanese co-member and no local shareholder is required. Master recordings, compositions and trade marks remain owned in New Zealand, and the Japanese company operates under an exclusive Japan territory licence, remitting licence fees back here. If the Japanese operation ever stopped trading, the catalogue would not be inside it.

Rights and cultural protocol will be documented rather than assumed. Agreements with our partners will cover language use, cultural review, rights division and mana over the work. A cross Indigenous creative protocol will be published from that, so other Māori artists can enter international collaborations with a protocol rather than a guess.

The work itself is the vehicle. Eight waiata carry the programme: two released and six in development, each with its own focus so that each can stand as its own release and carry its own future production application. They are Kotahi te kaupapa, the kaupapa itself and the Japan launch single; Kua muia e te pouritanga, the flute song with kōauau and shakuhachi in dialogue; Tapuwae tipuna, on intergenerational transmission; Mokemoke, on loneliness and language loss; Te Ara Moana, on the voyaging connection between the Pacific and Japan; and Rua Reo, on what two language communities owe each other.

Year one, 2027. Establish the Japanese vehicle and formalise the partnership in writing. Compose and record four waiata. Release Kotahi te kaupapa as the Japan launch single with a bilingual campaign. Register the works with APRA and their Japanese counterparts. Publish the international strategy as a standing document.

Year two, 2028. Release Rua Reo and Mokemoke. Take the programme into the Japanese market in person, with two or more performances for Japanese industry in Tokyo and one regional centre, six or more meetings with Japanese labels, publishers and festival programmers, and a co-writing residency in Aotearoa hosting Ryo Suzuki and our wider partner network on Kahungunu and Ngāi Tahu whenua.

Year three, 2029. Release the full length collaborative album in both markets. Present at a major international festival. Publish the cross Indigenous creative protocol framework and hand the model to a second Māori act, so the pathway outlives this grant.

Success will be measured by: the entity registered at 100 percent New Zealand ownership with agreements signed and articles filed; four waiata recorded and registered in 2027; the launch single released with local collection in place; two or more performances and six or more industry meetings in 2028; the album released in both markets in 2029; and every release culturally reviewed and signed off before release.

We are requesting $75,000 per year for three calendar years, $225,000 in total, for a fund period of 1 January 2027 to 31 December 2029.
